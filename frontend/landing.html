The problem

A severity score is not a consequence

Scanners rank bugs by CVSS. CVSS says how bad a bug is anywhere. It does not know which hospital loses power, how many people lose water, or how many minutes the defenders have.

    Water anomaly ML

    An attack nobody has seen before still looks abnormal

    Vulnerability feeds only know published weaknesses. Our water model, built by Suryans Padhy, learned how a water network normally behaves. When tanks and pressures stop matching what the pumps imply, it raises an alert.

    Historical replay, BATADAL dataset04 (2016). Not live telemetry.
    Anomaly score (0-100)Alert threshold (50)Alert hours
    4/4held-out attacks detected (evaluated once)
    1.37false alarms per 7 days
    0.95precision on the held-out test
    Layer 1: ML (Model C)

    Predicts each tank level and pressure from the last hours and the pump settings. A big miss is unusual. Fast: 0-3 hours.

    Layer 2: safety rules

    Hard limits, pump-flow consistency, rate of change, frozen sensors. Slower, but raised no false alarm in the replay.

    Questions

    What judges usually ask

    Is the data real?

    The city is simulated: its systems, links and services were designed for this project. The vulnerabilities are real: CVEs from NVD matched to real products, with CISA's Known Exploited list and FIRST EPSS scores. The water data is the public BATADAL benchmark, replayed historically.

    Why did you not train your own CVE exploitation model?

    FIRST EPSS already predicts exploitation from far more data than we could collect. We use EPSS and KEV for that, and spent our machine learning on a question EPSS cannot answer: is the physical process behaving abnormally? Six sector models were built against gates fixed before training. Two passed and are in the platform: water anomaly detection (BATADAL) and electricity attack screening (MSU-ORNL, high precision, about 40% coverage). Healthcare, telecom and transport failed their gates; for IoT no honest test was possible (no session data), so it is shown only as a labelled demonstration benchmark. All six are in the platform's Model evaluation page with every criterion and score.

    How are attack times decided?

    Each link has a base crossing time, slowed by firewalls and segmentation and sped up by how breakable the target is. These are assumptions, listed in one file, and the platform moves every one of them 30% down and up to check that the fix order still holds.

    How does the failsafe decide?

    It does not decide alone. An alarm proposes protective actions and waits for a human. Only if nobody answers within the set time does it cut internet and vendor access. Every step, human or automatic, goes into an audit log, and only a human can undo it.

    What if the attacker never touches a monitored system?

    Then detection is slow (about four hours in the model), and the platform shows exactly that: many response deadlines fall before anyone would notice. That gap is the argument for the monitoring fixes the optimiser recommends.

    Does it scan or attack real systems?

    No. Everything is an abstract simulation of the modelled city. There is no scanner, no exploit code and no connection to real infrastructure.
